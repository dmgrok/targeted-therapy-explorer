Steps people take today for personalised cancer treatment

Each step has a short explanation, a static box on how AI can help you digest this and what it must not do, and sources. The path is the same wherever you live. Where to start looking lists official places in France, Switzerland and the EU.

Some figures come from studies of older AI models; dates are shown with each source.

The process

Personalised cancer care uses tests on the tumour’s genes, proteins and other markers. The aim is to choose a treatment more likely to help. The U.S. National Cancer Institute (NCI) says biomarker testing is already routine to select treatment in some cancers. It names non-small cell lung cancer, breast cancer and colorectal cancer. The same page says this “is not yet part of routine care for most patients.” That page was last updated on 14 December 2021. A result is a snapshot.

Step 1 of 7

Diagnosis and pathology

A pathologist studies tissue from a biopsy or from surgery. Their report is the definitive diagnosis. You can ask to discuss the report with your doctor.

How AI can help — and its limits

AI can turn hard words into a short glossary. It can help you list questions for the visit.

It must not interpret the report before your doctor has seen it.

Sources for this step
  • NCI, Pathology reports. Reviewed 8 August 2022. Opened 13 November 2026 (Europe/Paris).

Step 2 of 7

Molecular testing

Doctors can test tumour tissue, or a blood sample (a liquid biopsy), for gene and protein markers. In France, national hospital platforms run many of these tests. A result is a snapshot. It may point to a drug, a trial, or neither. The decision is with your care team.

How AI can help — and its limits

AI can explain types of tests. It can help you prepare questions.

It must not predict a result. It must not match a person to a drug.

Sources for this step
  • NCI, Biomarker testing for cancer treatment. Page last updated 14 December 2021. Opened 13 November 2026 (Europe/Paris).
  • INCa, Plateformes de génétique moléculaire des cancers. 11 February 2022; directory from 2020. Opened 13 November 2026 (Europe/Paris).

Step 3 of 7

Second opinion

Asking for a second opinion is common. France’s national cancer institute calls it an important step. You can take your reports and questions to another specialist.

How AI can help — and its limits

AI can help you organise reports and questions.

AI is not a second opinion. It must not pick a doctor for you.

Sources for this step
  • NCI, Finding cancer care. Last updated 28 August 2024. Includes second opinion. Opened 13 November 2026 (Europe/Paris).
  • INCa, La réunion de concertation pluridisciplinaire. 25 July 2025. Opened 13 November 2026 (Europe/Paris).

Step 4 of 7

Tumour board

In many centres, a group of specialists meets to discuss a person’s file. This is a tumour board. In France it is called a réunion de concertation pluridisciplinaire, or RCP. Official French guidance says every person with cancer should have their file presented when treatment starts or changes.

In Switzerland, a quality label for breast centres requires a tumour board. That label is for breast centres only. Some centres also hold a molecular review of gene-test results. France’s rare-cancer networks include an expert board. An ESMO patient guide from 2011, which is dated, calls a tumour board common in many centres. This page links to professional recommendations. It does not copy them.

How AI can help — and its limits

AI can explain what the meeting is for.

It must not second-guess the board. It must not take the place of a molecular review.

Step 5 of 7

Matching to options or trials

A gene-test result may point to an approved drug, a use not listed on the label, or a clinical trial. The decision is made with the treating doctor. This site’s drug pages sit at this step. They are general information from public sources. They are not a recommendation.

Step 6 of 7

Monitoring and re-testing

Other markers can follow cancer during and after treatment. Markers can change. If the cancer comes back, the team may want to test again.

How AI can help — and its limits

AI can help you list questions for a follow-up visit.

It must not read a scan or a lab file and declare news. It must not advise a new biopsy.

Sources for this step

Step 7 of 7

Access routes

Some medicines can be reached before a full authorisation, under official early-access or compassionate-use rules. In France, accès précoce (AAP) is run by HAS, with four stated conditions. Accès compassionnel (AAC) is a named-patient route run by ANSM. In the EU, the EMA describes compassionate use, which Member States run. Public trial finders are listed in Where to start looking.

How AI can help — and its limits

AI can say which kind of route a public record describes. It can point to the official page.

It must not say that a person qualifies.

Limits on every AI use

The figures in this first paragraph are from 2023 studies of GPT-3.5, an older model. They may not describe current tools.

A JAMA Oncology letter, published online 24 August 2023, found chatbot responses “hallucinated (ie, were not part of any recommended treatment) in 13 of 104 (12.5%) outputs,” and that “one-third of treatments recommended by the chatbot were at least partially nonconcordant with NCCN guidelines.” The authors wrote: “Clinicians should advise patients that LLM chatbots are not a reliable source of treatment information.” NCI, on 3 October 2023, quoted the lead author: chatbots can “seamlessly combine correct and incorrect treatment recommendations, making it difficult even for experts to detect the errors.” Those studies used GPT-3.5 and peers.

A later, peer-reviewed study was published on 23 October 2024 in JAMA Network Open. It tested 10 chatbots, including later models, on 79 clinical oncology cases. On multiple-choice questions, the best score was 57 out of 79 (72%). On free-text answers, which are closer to a real conversation, the best score was 30 out of 79 (38%). Nine of the 10 chatbots, including all three that could read images, scored lower on free-text than on multiple-choice. The authors wrote that more work is needed to improve reliability in oncology. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.37711.

Do not paste identifiable records into a public tool. This site has no forms that collect anything, and no live AI in this version.

  • NCI Cancer Currents, “Can Artificial Intelligence–Driven Chatbots Correctly Answer Questions about Cancer?”. 3 October 2023, Edward Winstead. Re-opened 13 November 2026 (Europe/Paris).
  • JAMA Network Open, “Performance of Multimodal Artificial Intelligence Chatbots Evaluated on Clinical Oncology Cases”. Published 23 October 2024. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.37711. Opened 14 November 2026 (Europe/Paris).

One person’s story: Sid Sijbrandij

This is one person’s story, not evidence that a treatment works. It is not medical advice, a recommendation, or a ranking. The people on these pages are not options in the tables. This is n=1, not a template to copy.

Sid Sijbrandij has bone cancer, called osteosarcoma, in the spine. A longer telling, with a timeline, is on his case study page.

In his own words

After I ran out of standard-of-care treatment options and there were no trials available for me, I started doing maximum diagnostics, created new treatments, started doing treatments in parallel, and began scaling this for others.

Per his cancer page.

In an OpenAI Forum talk, posted 18 March 2026, he said AI tools helped them work through enormous amounts of data and navigate new treatments. The talk describes ChatGPT, then expert review. A collaborator, Jacob Stern, said there, of a custom agent harness: “I’m not gonna trust this out of the box, it can certainly make mistakes.”

Per the OpenAI Forum on-page transcript, posted 18 March 2026.

I currently have no evidence of disease.

But my fight with cancer is far from over.

Per his Substack post of 20 January 2026.

As of 20 January 2026, per his Substack post.

The pattern worth taking is speed of digestion, under the care team. It is never a system that chooses treatment.
